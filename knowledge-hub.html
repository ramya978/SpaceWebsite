Space Science Glossary

Clear definitions of terms from astronomy, cosmology, spacecraft, planetary science and space technology.

Apogee
The point in an orbit farthest from the body being orbited.
Astronomical unit (AU)
The average distance from Earth to the Sun, about 149.6 million km.
Black hole
A region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape once inside the event horizon.
CubeSat
A small standardised satellite built from 10 cm cubic units, widely used for education and low-cost missions.

Hohmann transfer
An efficient two-burn orbit change between two circular orbits.
In-situ resource utilisation
Using materials found on the Moon, Mars or asteroids to make propellant, water or building materials.
Kessler syndrome
A chain reaction of collisions in orbit that creates more debris and makes some orbits unusable.
Light-year
The distance light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion km.
Low Earth orbit (LEO)
Orbits below about 2,000 km altitude, used by the International Space Station and many Earth-observation satellites.
Microgravity
The condition of near-weightlessness experienced in orbit, where objects are in continuous free fall.
Perigee
The point in an orbit closest to the body being orbited.
Redshift
The stretching of light to longer wavelengths, used to measure how fast distant galaxies recede.
Regolith
The layer of loose dust and broken rock covering the surface of the Moon, Mars and asteroids.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about space, missions and technology, and the difference between evidence, active research and theory.

How far away is the Moon?

About 384,400 km on average. Light takes roughly 1.3 seconds to travel that far.

How many planets are in the Solar System?

Eight: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.

How big is the universe?

The observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across. The whole universe may be larger, or infinite.

Is there life elsewhere?

We have not found it yet. Astrobiology studies where and how to look, from Mars and ocean moons to planets around other stars.

Can I see satellites with my eyes?

Yes. The International Space Station is often brighter than any star and crosses the sky in a few minutes.
